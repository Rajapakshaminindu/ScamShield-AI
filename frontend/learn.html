GUIDE 01 · MESSAGE SAFETY

How to check a suspicious message

A message can look polished and still be fraudulent. Scammers copy company names, logos, writing styles, and even real details from public sources. Instead of trying to judge a message by appearance alone, look for what it asks you to do and verify the request independently.

Pause when you see these pressure tactics

  • Urgency or threats: a demand to act immediately to avoid arrest, account closure, a fine, or loss of a delivery.
  • Unexpected rewards: a prize, refund, job, or investment return that requires an upfront fee or sensitive information.
  • Requests for secrets: one-time codes, passwords, card security codes, recovery phrases, or remote access. Treat an unsolicited request for these as a serious warning.
  • Moving the conversation: pressure to continue on a private messaging app, keep the matter secret, or avoid speaking with your bank or family.
  • Unusual payment methods: gift cards, cryptocurrency, person-to-person transfers, or a new bank account presented as the only way to pay.

Verify without using the message's contact details

Do not reply, click its links, or call a number included in a message you are unsure about. Open the official app you already use, type the institution's known website address yourself, or call the number printed on your bank card or a previous statement. Ask whether the alert or payment request is genuine. A real organization should allow you to verify through its normal support channels.

If the message came from someone you know, contact them using a different method or a number you already had saved. Their account may have been taken over. Preserve a screenshot if you need to report it, but hide account numbers, addresses, and other private details before sharing it.

Simple rule: an unexpected request for money or login codes deserves an independent check, even if the sender name looks familiar.

GUIDE 02 · LINK CHECKS

How to inspect a link without opening it

Links can hide the destination behind a shortened address, a look-alike spelling, or a subdomain that puts a trusted brand name in the wrong place. A padlock or HTTPS means the connection is encrypted; it does not prove that the website is legitimate.

  1. Preview the full address. On a computer, hover over the link without clicking. On a phone, press and hold to preview it, taking care not to open it.
  2. Read the registered domain from right to left. In secure.example.com.attacker.test, the controlling domain is attacker.test, not example.com. Extra words before a real domain do not make it official.
  3. Look for misleading details. Watch for misspellings, added hyphens, unexpected country-code endings, letter substitutions, and domains unrelated to the organization.
  4. Use a separate route instead. If an account needs attention, open its official app or enter a known address yourself rather than following a message link.
  5. Do not paste private links into public tools. A link can contain password-reset tokens, invitation codes, or personal identifiers. Remove those parts or avoid submitting the URL.

When checking a domain, consider whether it matches a source you already trust; do not rely on a risk score alone. Newly created sites and compromised legitimate sites may not yet appear on reputation lists. If money or credentials are involved, verify with the organization directly regardless of a scanner's result.

GUIDE 03 · DAMAGE LIMITATION

If you clicked, shared information, or sent money

Act promptly, but do not panic. The right next step depends on what happened. Opening a page is different from entering a password, installing an app, or approving a transfer. Stop communicating with the sender and use a trusted device or contact channel for recovery.

If you entered a password or verification code

  • Visit the genuine service directly and change the exposed password. If you reused it elsewhere, change those passwords too.
  • Sign out other sessions, review recovery email addresses and phone numbers, and turn on multi-factor authentication where available.
  • Contact your bank immediately through its official number if you shared banking credentials, a card number, or a one-time code.

If you installed an app or gave remote access

  • Disconnect the device from the internet if you suspect active remote control; use another trusted device to contact your bank and important services.
  • Remove unfamiliar remote-access or sideloaded apps, run the device maker's security checks, and ask a trusted technician for help if needed.
  • Review recent account sessions and transactions. Do not use a phone number or support link supplied by the suspected caller.

If you sent money

Call the sending bank or payment provider's fraud team as soon as possible and ask whether the transfer can be stopped or recalled. Save transaction references, messages, phone numbers, and timestamps. Report the incident to the relevant local law-enforcement or consumer-protection authority. Reporting routes vary by country; use the official government website for your location. Do not pay a stranger who promises to recover lost funds for an advance fee.

Keep records and follow the provider's instructions. No scanner or adviser can guarantee that a payment will be recovered, but an early report may help the institution act quickly.

GUIDE 04 · TOOL TRANSPARENCY

What ScamShield's analysis can—and cannot—tell you

ScamShield combines pattern-based checks with an optional language-model service. It looks for signals such as pressure to act, requests for sensitive information, and suspicious wording or domains. A result is an automated assessment of the information supplied, not a confirmation of who sent it or whether a transaction is safe.

Automated checks can miss new scams and can flag legitimate messages. A low-risk or “safe” result is not a guarantee. Do not use the tool as the only basis for sending money, sharing credentials, installing software, or ignoring a genuine account issue. Confirm important claims using the institution's own app or independently obtained contact details.

Text and URLs can be checked with local pattern rules when no AI provider is configured. Image analysis requires the configured vision-model service; without it, image analysis is unavailable. Audio transcription and analysis are not currently supported. Never submit passwords, one-time codes, full card details, private recovery links, or information you are not comfortable sharing with the service provider described in the Privacy section.

Need to act now? Contact your bank or the affected service directly. For an emergency, use your local emergency or law-enforcement channel—not this website.

Three habits that reduce risk

  1. Pause before acting on unexpected urgency.
  2. Verify with a known app, website, or phone number.
  3. Never share a one-time code or password with a caller or message sender.

Important limitation

ScamShield is an educational aid. Automated results can be incomplete or wrong; they are not professional, legal, banking, or emergency advice.
